# Portfolio Copy — Tone & Voice Guide

Source material: Noto case study, Omro (device/app) case study.
Use case: Writing and reviewing all case study copy on this portfolio going forward.

---

## NON-NEGOTIABLE: NO EM DASHES

**Never use em dashes (—) in case study copy. Not in headings, not in body text, not anywhere.**

Replace every em dash with a period, a comma, or restructure the sentence. Always. No exceptions.

✗ "The feature filled a gap — users were already looking for it."
✓ "The feature filled a gap. Users were already looking for it."

✗ "Every edge case needed a clear response — users should always know what's happening."
✓ "Every edge case needed a clear response, so users always know what's happening."

✗ "Both things needed solving — the excitement and the trust."
✓ "Both things needed solving: the excitement and the trust."

This rule exists because em dashes keep appearing in drafts. They are banned. Use a period, use a comma, use a colon, or rewrite the sentence. There is no situation in case study copy where an em dash is the right choice.

## What makes this voice distinct

Across both reference case studies, five things define the voice:

1. **It starts from a person, not a product.** The writer is present — in the first sentence, often in the first clause. The work is described through the lens of someone who cared about it and had opinions while doing it.

2. **It's direct but not declarative.** Sentences land. They don't hedge or qualify everything, but they also don't sound like press releases. The writing makes claims and owns them.

3. **It mixes registers on purpose.** A precise design observation sits next to a casual aside. A data point sits next to "but the thing is." This isn't sloppy, it's how the writer actually thinks. The informality is honest, not performed.

4. **It trusts short sentences.** Three words can end a paragraph. This isn't about minimalism for its own sake — it's about rhythm. Short sentences punch. Long sentences carry weight. The mix creates a pace that feels alive.

5. **It admits things.** Limitations, uncertainty, personal preference, what didn't get done. The references don't oversell. "While this was just a school project," "Juggling other projects limited the time I wish I had," "(before it broke RIP)." This honesty makes the writing feel real.

## Patterns to apply

### 1. Open sections from personal observation, not from the field

The references don't start with market framing. They start with what the writer noticed or experienced.

**Do this:**
> "I've used my notes app for years, and it's become a trusty but chaotic archive of my life, from 2am thoughts, to-dos, and mind dumps."
> — Noto, The Problem

> "As a group of designers curious about how people connect today, we set out to understand what authentic socialization means in a world of performative interactions."
> — Omro, Overview

**Not this:**
> "Presales are a genuinely valuable feature for a crypto wallet. They give users early access to new tokens, often at a better entry price than after launch, and they drive real community engagement."
> — Best Wallet, The Problem (current draft)

The BW version opens with a market claim. The references open with a person. When no personal observation exists, use a concrete, specific fact or a direct question — not a category summary.

---

### 2. Use "I wanted to" instead of "the design had to" or "the goal was to"

The references are written by the designer, not by the design. Agency belongs to the person.

**Do this:**
> "I wanted to create a space where insights emerge naturally, not because users are trying to hit a quota."
> — Noto, Opportunity

> "I wanted to create something deeply personal, bringing together my favorite things."
> — Noto, Overview

**Not this:**
> "The design had to make buying in feel genuinely worth it."
> — Best Wallet, The Problem (current draft)

> "My job was making sure that information was visible and easy to understand..."
> ← This one works because it's still first-person with a personal frame. The problem is specifically "the design had to" or "the goal was to" constructions that erase the writer.

---

### 3. Short sentences carry their own weight

Both references use one- and two-sentence kickers to land a point. These aren't transitions — they're punctuation on a thought.

**Do this:**
> "We surveyed 57 people. 47 said they don't talk to someone they're curious about simply because they 'never cross paths.'"
> — Omro, The Problem

> "But the thing is they do cross paths."
> — Omro, The Problem

> "No profiles to scroll. Just a quiet device that notices patterns you can't see, and sparks connections when the moment is right."
> — Omro, Solution

> "This was a feature worth building."
> ← This BW line actually works.

**Not this:**
> "The answer was hierarchy, not reduction."
> — Best Wallet, Design Challenge (current draft)

That last one *sounds* good but is designed to sound good — it's a quotable kicker written to impress, not to land a genuine thought. The reference voice earns its short sentences by making real observations, not by constructing aphorisms.

---

### 4. Use "you" to put the reader inside the experience

In core flow descriptions and product explanations, the references address the reader directly. "You" makes the product feel real and concrete.

**Do this:**
> "Once you're nearby, use your Omro as a compass that points you toward each other."
> — Omro, Core Flows

> "Return to past personas to see how you've changed."
> — Noto, Core Flows

> "Ask questions without digging, like 'What have I been stressed about?'"
> — Noto, Core Flows

**Not this:**
> "User selects a non-Ethereum token as payment. The app shows a warning banner, greys out Buy & Stake, and keeps Buy active."
> — Best Wallet, Edge Cases (current draft)

That's documentation language. It describes a user in third person. The reference voice would say "You select" or "If you don't have enough ETH for gas..." or use a first-person narrator explaining their own design logic.

---

### 5. Allow honest asides

The references let personal observations slip in — imperfections, personal preferences, small admissions. These make the writing feel lived-in.

**Do this:**
> "Personally, I found many existing journaling apps overwhelming, which is why I kept returning to my Notes app."
> — Noto, UX Strategy

> "Juggling other projects limited the time I wish I had to refine the interactions and the UI."
> — Noto, Next Steps

> "This is a tool I'd love to use myself."
> — Noto, Next Steps

> "(before it broke RIP)"
> — Omro, Form Ideation

These don't undermine credibility — they establish it. The reader trusts a writer who admits limitations more than one who presents everything as intentional and polished.

**Not this:**
> "The file handed over with component-level behavior annotations and interaction specs for engineering."
> — Best Wallet, Next Steps (current draft)

Passive, impersonal. No one is in this sentence.

---

### 6. Section titles take a position

The references don't use section titles to summarize what the section contains. They use them to make a claim, ask a question, or land an observation.

**Do this:**
> "No one is building for self-insight, only self-documentation."
> — Noto, The Problem title

> "We're more connected than ever. So why does it feel so lonely?"
> — Omro, The Problem title

> "People don't need more prompts, they need meaning."
> — Noto, Opportunity title

**Not this:**
> "Designing a presale purchase flow that didn't exist yet."
> — Best Wallet, The Problem title (current draft)

That's a neutral description of a situation. The references would frame it as a claim about the world that this project was responding to.

---

### 7. Contractions are default

The references use contractions throughout without exception. Don't = don't. Isn't = isn't. That's = that's. I'd = I'd. Every time a contraction is avoided, the sentence sounds formal where it should sound natural.

**Both references use:** don't, isn't, it's, they're, you're, I'd, we'd, couldn't, wasn't, won't, that's, I've, I'm.

Scan every sentence: if there's a place for a contraction and it's not there, add it.

---

### 8. Paragraph length stays short

Both references stay under 4 sentences per paragraph almost without exception. When a paragraph runs long, it's usually one that should be two.

Long paragraphs signal that the writer is trying to cover too much ground in one breath. Split them. Let each thought land before starting the next one.

---

### 9. Core flow descriptions explain the experience, not the UI

The references describe what the flow *does for the person*, not what elements appear on screen.

**Do this:**
> "A simple place for your thoughts. Plans, reflections, or brain dumps — write whatever calls to you in the moment."
> — Noto, 01 Text Entry

> "Zoom seamlessly from a high-level summary of who you are to the writing behind it. Every insight and pattern traces back to the original notes that created it."
> — Noto, 06 Navigation

**Not this:**
> "Live countdown, price badge, fundraising progress, and a sticky buy widget."
> — Best Wallet, Happy Path caption (current draft)

That's a UI inventory. The reference voice would describe what that screen *does* — why the countdown is there, what the progress bar communicates, what the widget invites you to do.

---

### 10. What I Learned sections are humble and specific

The references don't frame lessons as universal truths. They frame them as things the writer personally understood for the first time, or understood differently after this project.

**Do this:**
> "While this was just a school project, I approached it like a product I'd pitch."
> — Noto, What I Learned

> "The hardest part isn't building a great product — it's getting enough people to use it that it becomes valuable."
> — Omro, What I Learned

> "I learned to think about how the physical device, digital interface, and brand narrative all work together — not as separate deliverables, but as one cohesive experience."
> — Omro, What I Learned

These are honest, specific to the project, and admit something was new to the writer. They don't overgeneralize.

## Patterns to avoid (with examples from current Best Wallet draft)

### Polished kickers designed to sound quotable

These are lines that are constructed to impress, not to communicate. They feel like the sentence was written to be the pull quote, not to complete a thought.

> "The answer was hierarchy, not reduction."
> "Trust holds or breaks."
> "This was a feature worth building."

The last one is acceptable because it's plain. The first two reach for a rhetorical effect that's slightly out of character with the rest of the voice.

---

### Market framing in place of personal framing

> "Presales are a genuinely valuable feature for a crypto wallet. They give users early access to new tokens, often at a better entry price than after launch, and they drive real community engagement."

"Drive real community engagement" is marketing language. The references never read like someone pitching a product to investors. Replace with a personal reason to care, or a concrete observation.

---

### "The design" or "the goal" as subject instead of "I"

> "The design had to make buying in feel genuinely worth it."
> "The goal was to reveal patterns and reflections..."

These erase the writer. The references say "I wanted," "I found," "we decided," "I knew." The designer is accountable for the decisions.

---

### Third-person user ("the user", "someone") in flow descriptions

> "User selects a non-Ethereum token as payment. The app shows a warning banner..."
> "User enters more than their token balance allows."

The references address the reader as "you" or describe the experience from inside it. Third-person "the user" reads as a spec doc, not a case study.

---

### Passive phrasing that removes the writer

> "The file handed over with component-level behavior annotations and interaction specs for engineering."

Who handed it over? Who wrote the annotations? The writer was there — put them in the sentence.

---

### Abstract claims in place of concrete details

> "They drive real community engagement."
> "The design had to make buying in feel genuinely worth it."

The references use specific examples to make abstract ideas concrete: "the person you keep seeing at the coffee shop," "Spotify Wrapped-style self-discovery," "2am thoughts." When the writing goes abstract, ask: what's the real-world version of this claim?

---

### Jargon that isn't explained

> "in a format a non-expert can actually scan"

"Non-expert" is jargon for "someone who doesn't know crypto." Say that instead. The references explain technical things in plain terms or skip the jargon entirely: "No profiles to curate, no swiping, no awkward DMs."

---

## Self-check checklist

Before finalizing any case study section, run it against this list. Every item should pass.

- [ ] **Does the first sentence come from a person, not a product or market category?**
- [ ] **Are there contractions everywhere one would naturally appear?** ("don't," "isn't," "I'd," "it's," "that's")
- [ ] **Is there at least one short, punchy sentence in the section?** (Under 10 words.)
- [ ] **Is the writer present?** At least one "I" or "we" that reflects a real decision or observation.
- [ ] **Is there any "the design had to" or "the goal was to" that should be "I wanted to"?** Replace it.
- [ ] **Are core flow descriptions written from inside the experience ("you"), not as UI inventory?**
- [ ] **Are there any polished aphorisms that feel designed to impress?** ("trust holds or breaks," "the answer was hierarchy, not reduction.") If so, rewrite as a plain observation.
- [ ] **Does any paragraph run longer than 4 sentences?** If so, consider splitting it.
- [ ] **Is there anything specific and concrete?** A named element, a number, a real-world example. If the whole section is abstract, add one.
- [ ] **Does the What I Learned section admit something new or imperfect?** If it reads as a general truth rather than a specific realization, rewrite it.
- [ ] **Is there any third-person "the user" in a section that should use "you"?** Change it.
- [ ] **Are there any passive sentences where the writer could be the subject?** ("The file handed over" → "I handed over the file")
